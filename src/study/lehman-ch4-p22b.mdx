Reversing the arrows of a relation exchanges its incoming and outgoing conditions. In this exercise, a function has at most one arrow leaving each input. A total function has exactly one. A surjective function in this sense may be undefined at some inputs.

<Exercise id="lehman-ch4-p22b" sourceLabel="Chapter 4 — Problem 22(b)">

<Solution id="lehman-ch4-p22b-solution" title="Surjection/injection equivalence">

The selected source states the claim:

<Equation id="lehman-ch4-p22b-claim" tag="p22bclaim">

$$
A \surj B \iff B \inj A
$$

</Equation>

<Proof id="lehman-ch4-p22b-proof" title="Proof by inverse relations">

The source proves the claim through two implications:

<Equation id="lehman-ch4-p22b-lemma1" tag="p22blemma1">

$$
A \surj B \implies B \inj A
$$

</Equation>

<Equation id="lehman-ch4-p22b-lemma2" tag="p22blemma2">

$$
B \inj A \implies A \surj B
$$

</Equation>

<Subproof id="lehman-ch4-p22b-lemma1-proof" title="Proof of p22blemma1">

The proof is by contradiction. Suppose that $A \surj B$ is true but
$B \inj A$ is false. Then there is a surjective function
$f : A \rightarrow B$.

Suppose that the inverse relation $f^{-1}: B \rightarrow A$ is not total.
Then there is a $b \in B$ such that no arrows of $f^{-1}$ originate from
$b$. This would imply that no arrows of $f$ terminate at $b$, contradicting
that $f$ is surjective. Hence, $f^{-1}$ must be total.

Suppose that $f^{-1}: B \rightarrow A$ is not injective. Then there is an
$a \in A$ such that more than one arrow of $f^{-1}$ terminates at $a$. This
would imply that more than one arrow of $f$ originates at $a$, contradicting
that $f$ is a function. Hence, $f^{-1}$ must be injective.

Since $f^{-1}$ is both total and injective, it is an injective total relation.
Thus the supposition that $B \inj A$ is false is contradicted, and
<EquationRef id="lehman-ch4-p22b-lemma1">p22blemma1</EquationRef> holds.

</Subproof>

<Subproof id="lehman-ch4-p22b-lemma2-proof" title="Proof of p22blemma2">

The proof is by contradiction. Suppose that $B \inj A$ is true but
$A \surj B$ is false. Then there is an injective total relation
$g : B \rightarrow A$.

Suppose that $g^{-1}: A \rightarrow B$ is not surjective. Then there is a
$b \in B$ such that no arrows of $g^{-1}$ terminate at $b$. This would imply
that no arrows of $g$ originate at $b$, contradicting that $g$ is total.
Hence, $g^{-1}$ must be surjective.

Suppose that $g^{-1}$ is not a function. Then there is an $a \in A$ such
that more than one arrow of $g^{-1}$ originates at $a$. This would imply
that more than one arrow of $g$ terminates at $a$, contradicting that $g$ is
injective. Hence, $g^{-1}$ must be a function.

Since $g^{-1}$ is both surjective and a function, it is a surjective
function. Thus the supposition that $A \surj B$ is false is contradicted, and
<EquationRef id="lehman-ch4-p22b-lemma2">p22blemma2</EquationRef> holds.

</Subproof>

The two implications are established, so
<EquationRef id="lehman-ch4-p22b-claim">p22bclaim</EquationRef> follows.

</Proof>

</Solution>
