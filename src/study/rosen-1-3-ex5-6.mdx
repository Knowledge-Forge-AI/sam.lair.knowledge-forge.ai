## Worked example: when an implication is false

<Proof id="rosen-1-3-negated-implication-proof" title="Derive the negation of an implication">

Replace $q$ with $\neg q$ in the equivalence between
<EquationRef id="rosen-1-3-ex6-left">Exercise 6’s negated conjunction</EquationRef>
and <EquationRef id="rosen-1-3-ex6-right">its disjunction of negations</EquationRef>.
Double negation removes the two negations on $q$. The resulting disjunction
is the truth-functional definition of $p \to q$.

<Equation id="rosen-1-3-implication-derivation" tag="Implication">

$$
\begin{aligned}
\neg(p \land \neg q)
  &\equiv \neg p \lor \neg\neg q \\
  &\equiv \neg p \lor q \\
  &\equiv p \to q
\end{aligned}
$$

</Equation>

Negate both ends of the displayed equivalence and simplify the resulting
double negation.

$$
\neg(p \to q) \equiv p \land \neg q
$$

An implication is false exactly when its premise is true and its
conclusion is false. For example, $p = \mathrm{false}$ cannot falsify it
regardless of the value assigned to $q$.

</Proof>
